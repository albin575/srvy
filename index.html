01

Introduction

Money management is a skill that most young people are expected to learn on their own. By the time a student leaves college, they are already handling a bank account, a UPI app and the family's expectations about saving — often without any formal training in how to do it well.

This study examines how college students in Piravom Municipality save and invest, and how those two habits reflect their overall financial awareness. The logic of the study is simple and sequential: saving is the first step of financial discipline, investment is the second, and awareness is the final result.

Awareness here is not measured as general knowledge alone. It is measured as knowledge that shows up in behaviour — budgeting regularly, keeping a fixed part of income aside, understanding interest, and knowing where money can be placed so that it grows safely.

02

Objectives of the Study

Objective 1

General level of financial literacy

To measure how far students understand budgeting, saving, interest rates, banking products and digital payments in everyday use.

Objective 2

Awareness of government schemes

To find out how many students know about schemes such as Jan Dhan Yojana, Mudra Yojana, Sukanya Samriddhi and Vaya Vandana Yojana.

Objective 3

Role of financial education campaigns

To assess how effective literacy programmes and campaigns are in reaching middle income youth, and how willing students are to attend them.

07

Findings

  • 72% of respondents have a saving habit, but only 28% invest regularly.
  • Students who save 10–30% of monthly income and invest in bank FD or gold scored 8+ marks.
  • Students who keep cash at home and hold no investment scored below 4 marks.
  • 34 out of 50 students have never attended any financial literacy programme.
  • Only 9 out of 50 are fully aware of how to report a digital payment fraud, even though 31 use digital payments daily.
  • Government scheme awareness is uneven — Jan Dhan Yojana is known to most, Vaya Vandana Yojana to very few.

“The survey found that 72% students have saving habit, but only 28% invest regularly. Students who save 10–30% monthly and invest in Bank FD/Gold scored high in financial awareness (8+ marks). Students who keep cash at home and have no investment scored low (below 4 marks). Hence, there is a direct relation: Regular Saving Habit + Correct Investment Pattern = Higher Financial Awareness. Lack of financial literacy is the main barrier. Financial awareness programs in colleges will improve saving and investment behavior.”

Final output statement — for the report and viva
08

Suggestions

  1. Compulsory financial literacy session in the first year

    A dedicated session during induction, before students open accounts and start using UPI, so that budgeting and saving come before spending.

  2. Encourage a ₹500 monthly SIP for students

    A small, affordable entry point into mutual funds through the college, so that investing becomes a habit rather than a later-life decision.

  3. Workshop on “How to avoid fake loan apps”

    Practical training on identifying fraudulent apps, checking RBI registration, and reporting a payment fraud within the golden hour.

09

Conclusion

Saving and investment are practical indicators of financial awareness. A student who sets aside a fixed part of his income every month, and places that money in a product that earns a return, is already applying what financial literacy teaches. A student who keeps cash at home is not.

The survey shows that awareness in Piravom Municipality is uneven: the habit of saving is widespread, but the knowledge of where to invest, which schemes exist and how to stay safe online is limited. Awareness is therefore not just theory — it is seen in habits. Regular saving combined with the correct investment pattern produces a higher level of financial awareness, and college-level programmes are the most direct way to close the gap.
